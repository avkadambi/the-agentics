│  training data sources. The EU AI Act also obliges providers to keep         │
│  **datasets and model documentation** for a minimum of five years, enabling  │

│  U.S., the **Federal Trade Commission (FTC)** has signaled that deceptive    │

│  AI‑specific negligence in legal contexts is still nascent. Thus, the        │
│  liability landscape is uncertain but trending toward greater                │

│  “legal skins”** for each market.                                            │

**Revised explainer (≈250 words)**  

AI tools that assist lawyers or judges must operate under a framework that keeps humans ultimately accountable.  Most existing AI regulations — notably the EU AI Act — classify “legal‑decision‑support” systems as **high‑risk** and require a qualified professional to review and approve any output before it influences a legal outcome.  This “human‑in‑the‑loop” principle is intended to prevent the delegation of discretionary authority to an algorithm.  

For high‑risk systems the EU Act obliges providers to furnish clear information about the system’s purpose, the data it was trained on, its performance, and an understandable explanation of each individual result.  The U.S. NIST AI Risk Management Framework echoes these transparency goals, although it remains a voluntary guidance rather than a binding rule [source needed].  

Empirical research (e.g., Barocas et al., 2022) shows that historical court records often embed racial and socioeconomic disparities.  Consequently, many regulators and industry best‑practice guidelines encourage documented bias‑assessment procedures, periodic audits, and the use of fairness‑aware modelling techniques, even though a uniform audit mandate is not yet in force [source needed].  

Independent advisory bodies such as the UK’s Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation provide policy advice and may review impact assessments, but they do not presently issue licences specific to legal‑sector AI [source needed].  France is discussing advisory mechanisms for AI in the legal sector, yet no formal “AI‑Legal Advisory Board” currently exists [source needed].  

Liability for erroneous AI advice presently falls under existing professional‑malpractice law: a lawyer or judge could be sued if they rely on an unreasonable recommendation without appropriate scrutiny.  At present, no dedicated statutes or case law expressly create a separate malpractice cause for AI‑generated advice, though the issue is under active discussion in many jurisdictions [source needed].
